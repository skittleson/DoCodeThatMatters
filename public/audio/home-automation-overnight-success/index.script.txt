Smart home devices should work without the internet. I wrote the first draft of this, AI helped me edit and rewrite it, and I made the final pass. The rules, setups, and talks are my own. Here is the short version: Every core smart device in my house does not depend on a vendor's server. If the internet goes down, everything works, though perhaps with a downgraded experience. It has been my rule for ten years, and it has been challenging to follow. I've given two talks about it, and I will walk you through each slide by slide.

Why local? My Nest integration worked one day until it didn't. They had changed the API around the same time Google acquired them. I have a few more examples in these talks that show a Samsung fridge and a grill controller that needs Wi-Fi to show temperature. I shouldn't need Wi-Fi and or a vendor service to interact with hardware. So, everything that matters runs on hardware I own. Mostly on Tasmota on ESP8266 or ESP32 boards, an MQTT broker, and Node-RED on a Raspberry Pi. I do use some cloud services for enhancements like Alexa and a few AWS ones, but those are secondary. If the internet is down, the switches all work like normal.

Let's look at the rules. From the first talk: First, no internet, no problem. Core stuff has to work with the internet unplugged. Second, don't remove the old way while adding the new one. Third, be agile. Fourth, check your automations. Fifth, exceptions must be exceptional.

Added after year five, once more people lived in the house: Sixth, a cloud device needs an API with logs or it doesn't get installed. I don't want a black box in the house. Seventh, configuration lives in one place instead of five vendor apps. Eighth, more than one way to do something, like an app, Alexa, a QR code, or an NFC tag. Ninth, if I keep doing the same steps, it becomes routine, either in Node-RED or on the Tasmota device itself. Tenth, devices talk pub-sub over MQTT instead of point to point. Eleventh, no batteries unless they last a year or more between changes.

I get asked about the Tasmota sprinkler controller the most, as well as the garage door setup. Both have been running for years with no real changes. You can find the link on the blog post if you want the details.

Now, Talk One: Home Automation. The title slide is "Home Automation, for fun and convenience." Nobody needs any of this. I like building things that just work in the background. Each automation is a small puzzle. It's a hobby.

The next slide is a collage of icons called "Connected Everything." It shows the modern everything-is-online mess. Everything seems to want to reach out to the cloud. Laptops, phones, etc., make sense. However, my thermostats and locks... smart devices need to be questioned since they could be critically useful in your home. They may go out of business, so be mindful of what you're signing up for.

Here is a slide showing a Nest thermostat, an Amazon smart plug, and a Yale smart lock keypad. This is the retail starting point: thermostat, smart plug, smart lock. Each can have its own account and app. Usually, people will try to combine devices into the same ecosystem when possible.

Next is a triangle diagram showing Privacy, Integration and Flexibility, and Cost. Know this triangle: a cloud cam is cheap and easy but in exchange for costs to your privacy. A smart plug I flash myself takes longer to set up, but I can hook it into anything. Figure out where a device lands before buying it.

This image shows a light switch taped on with a note reading "Leave Switch On. This is BAD." This is bad behavior. Don't tape switches to the on position.

The better fix is an in-wall Wi-Fi switch, like this Wemo Wi-Fi light switch shown here. It still works like a normal switch and I can automate it. It takes more work to install than a smart plug, but nobody has to change their habits.

Here is the slide with scissors cutting an ethernet cable under the words "No Internet, No Problem." Rule one: if the internet goes down, my lights still turn on.

The next slide shows sticky notes on a planning board titled "Must Be Agile." Being agile means willing to understand that changes will come that you must make in your system.

Here is a Node-RED flow for Garage Cool Off logic. It checks if the door is closed before running the fan. A small workflow to keep my garage cool in the summertime. I've had to change it multiple times.

This next slide shows a Node-RED garage sensor flow feeding Grafana, plus an opens counter and on-status chart. This slide shows the Node-RED logo, a Phillips Hue IFTTT-style applet, and an Alexa routine editor. Three ways to do "event in, action out." Node-RED is my main tool. Alexa routines and IFTTT-style applets cover the stuff I don't want to build myself.

This slide lists "Memorable Home Automations," which includes heart-rate light blinking, fireplace sim, rain-skip watering, phone-presence garage, AWS services, and 3D printer status lights. Talk Two: Home Automation, The Sequel. Enough time passed between the talks that I had a new list of problems. This slide is a meme of Marvin the android saying he could calculate your chance of survival but you won't like it. It's a nod to how much more is now automated. More devices means more decisions made without me. It's a little unsettling but mostly harmless.

The recap section reuses the "Connected Everything" slide from talk one. Then there is a slide showing IoT in the wild: a handwritten printer note, a fridge throwing a cert error, and a smart grill controller. Everyone else is dealing with this too, from what I can see.

The rules are restated: keep existing functionality, IoT must work without internet, be flexible, review periodically, exceptions must be exceptional. The rules stayed the same but more were added. Here is an Einstein quote: "We cannot solve our problems with the same thinking we used when we created them." My setup assumed one person lived in the house.

This slide lists some of those problems: more foot traffic, more people needing keys, longer trips away, and a water flooding scare. More people meant traffic pattern differences and additional access keys. Also, I would be longer away. So I needed two additional systems: water flooding detection and presence detection.

Here is the Captain Jack Sparrow meme again: "The Extended Rules: still guidelines." These are rules 6 to 11 at the top of the post: APIs with logs, central config, multiple interaction methods, routines, pub/sub, battery-life minimums.

Now the builds! This slide shows new builds: a motorized outdoor device, a plant grow light, and a closet UV sanitizing light. A motor and PVC pipe rig for outdoor lighting, a UV grow light clipped to a houseplant, and a UV strip under the cabinets for sanitizing.

The sprinkler build appears again, still running the same way years later. Then there are new sensors: a wall-mounted presence unit, a robot vacuum, and a DIY AirGradient air quality kit. Random automations with a water pump, AirGradient kit, and robot vacuum.

This is the wall dashboard: a wall-mounted tablet next to a Nest thermostat set to 80. This dashboard replaced more apps than anything else: garage door, side door unlock, front lock, all lights on or off, brightness, a coffee bar color picker, and warm-to-cool for the living room. It's one cheap tablet next to the thermostat. No one needs an app. It's a physical device everyone can go to if needed.

The lights behind the TV run through the same system instead of their own remote. This slide shows a large wall-mounted TV with warm backlighting glowing against the wall. These lights turn on when the TV comes on and shut off at smart times as well.

I do recommend smart locks when you need to have access controls in places other than just keys. This slide shows two generations of smart lock: a retrofit deadbolt and a full touchscreen keypad lock.

Check out the parts at the end of this post to see what I have used. Core compute includes an ESP32 D1 mini, a smart plug, and a Raspberry Pi 4. The ESP32 D1 mini handles anything custom (it's cheap). I've been able to find smart plugs I can flash with Tasmota. The Raspberry Pi runs Node-RED and Grafana.

For switching, I use LED strips for ambient lighting, power transistors for higher-current loads, and small relay modules so a microcontroller can switch mains voltage safely. The rest of the bin includes a resistor assortment, color-coded wire so I can trace a circuit later, and a capacitor kit for smoothing and timing.

The software stack is Node-RED for the logic, Grafana to see what's happening, Tasmota on anything I flash, Telegram for notifications, and Python or JavaScript for glue code that doesn't fit in a flow. My Garage flow today is crazy but it works and gets changed often.

This is the "is everything okay" dashboard: humidity, temperature, dew point, pressure and gas on one side, and energy use, door opens and on-status history on the other. I built it because I got tired of guessing.

Dashboards and Grafana panels work for me. Everyone else in the house just wants the light on. The "more than one way in" rule in hardware includes the wall tablet for whoever is standing there, NFC tags and a QR code to tap, and an Echo Dot for anyone who would rather talk to the house.

The tools I build and debug all of this with include a soldering iron for permanent connections, a 3D printer for enclosures and brackets you can't buy, a multimeter for when something isn't working, and a precision screwdriver kit for devices the manufacturer didn't want opened. That's both talks, five years apart. The rules barely changed. The list of problems kept growing. A third talk is coming, built around agentic use cases. The same rules should hold when an AI agent is the one flipping the switches, and I expect to add a few.

What Readers are telling me: I shared this post on LinkedIn and asked for the most pointless internet dependency people had found in a device. Dozens of people answered, then Hacker News commenters added more. Most examples were basic functions locked behind an app, an account, or a server. A fridge whose temperature can't be changed because of a bug in the manufacturer's phone app. A TV that can't even switch inputs when the internet is down, and another that has to be signed in before it works as a plain HDMI monitor. One reader's older smart TV has dead apps but still works as a regular TV, which is how a device should fail. A heating system whose app doesn't work from the local network. A kitchen vent and a dishwasher that need the internet even when you control them from Home Assistant. A smart plug that can't be set up on an isolated network. Noise-cancelling headphones that search for a Bluetooth device forever, so you can't just turn on the noise cancelling. Synthesizers and MIDI keyboards that need a cloud login for first use or firmware updates. A meat thermometer that only worked through its app and stopped working when the company went out of business.

The meat thermometer bothers me most. The hardware outlived the company. It stopped working anyway.

Rules readers added: Offline first, with the internet for extras. A device should do its main job on its own. Treat every third-party dependency as a risk. APIs change, so plan a local fallback or a clear degraded mode. One mobile developer works the same way: the core action works offline or from cache, and only the extras wait for a connection. Decide what must work offline. My answer is turning things on and off, temperature and safety controls, and anything a guest has to use without learning an app. As one reader said, if a visitor can't turn something on or adjust the temperature without an app account, the design has failed. A stricter version of my first two rules: everything core works with the Wi-Fi or the automation server shut down, and smart features never take away the dumb ones. The wall switch should control the light directly and let automation flip it. A switch that only sends a request to the automation fails. That reader says my sprinkler controller fails this bar too. The only sprinkler controller they'd found with a local API and full control on the device itself was RainMachine, which is gone. Clean up when you throw things away. Remove a connected printer from your account first. An abandoned connected device can be an easy way in for an attacker.

Practical tips: Use local protocols. One reader recommends Zigbee and Z-Wave over Wi-Fi, because a switch that doesn't need the cloud tends to behave the same year after year. Another uses Zigbee for the smart plugs that can't be set up without internet. Let an LLM find the firmware. One reader buys the cheapest hardware that does the job, ignores reviews that complain about the app, and asks an LLM for local-first custom firmware once it arrives. That has worked for their switches, a thermostat, an air quality monitor, a thermometer, and a window fan. They'd have paid about 2.5x for hardware that was local out of the box. Old Nest thermostats have a way out. No Longer Evil is open source firmware for Nest Gen 1 and Gen 2 thermostats. Smart TVs can be tamed. Reset the TV and never connect it to the network, and the input problem goes away. Remote access is the cost of staying local. I use Tailscale on the router and keep the individual devices off the tailnet. One reader ran OpenVPN on OpenWrt, which worked but felt clunky, and is now trying Tailscale. There's a gap to fill. One reader would happily pay for a pre-built, open "works with everything" Raspberry Pi image with MQTT. DietPi and OpenWrt come close and have a small footprint, but you need some understanding to get going. Another wanted a hub that ships with the house, like a water heater or a router. Home Assistant sells hub hardware that's close to that. Right to repair is the bigger fight. Readers pointed to the right-to-repair movement and groups like the FULU Foundation, which work to keep companies from locking you out of what you bought.

Counterpoints: Connectivity has real uses. A connected meat thermometer lets you watch the game while the steak cooks. The same reader also had an oven decide to update its software in the middle of cooking. One reader pointed out the business model behind all of this: companies would rather sell you a service than a product. Another expects the shift away from internet-connected devices to come from resilience worries rather than consumer complaints, since enough connected homes make a target. And one skips connected appliances entirely, keeping only computers and phones online.
