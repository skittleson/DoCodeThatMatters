From Hacker News, I know you don't like AI-written content, so I've reworked most of this to be in my own words. The slide decks are the most interesting part; LLMs were used to add context. To summarize: every core smart device in my house does not depend on a vendor's server. If the internet goes down, everything works, though perhaps with a downgraded experience. It has been my rule for ten years, and it has been challenging to follow. I've given two talks about it, and I'll walk you through each slide by slide.

**Why Local**

My Nest integration worked one day until it didn't. They had changed the API around the same time Google acquired them. I have a few more examples in these talks that show a Samsung fridge and a grill controller that needs Wi-Fi to show temperature. I don't need, and shouldn't need, Wi-Fi or a vendor service to interact with hardware.

So, everything that matters runs on hardware I own. Mostly on Tasmota on ESP8266 or ESP32 boards, an MQTT broker, and Node-RED on a Raspberry Pi. I do use some cloud services for enhancements like Alexa and a few AWS ones, but those are secondary. If the internet is down, the switches all work like normal.

**The Rules**

From the first talk, I established five rules:
First, no internet, no problem. Core stuff has to work with the internet unplugged.
Second, don't remove the old way while adding the new one.
Third, be agile.
Fourth, check your automations.
Fifth, exceptions must be exceptional.

I added five more after year five, once more people lived in the house:
Sixth, a cloud device needs an API with logs or it doesn't get installed. I don't want a black box in the house.
Seventh, configuration lives in one place instead of five vendor apps.
Eighth, more than one way to do something. An app, Alexa, a QR code, or an NFC tag.
Ninth, if I keep doing the same steps, it becomes routine, whether in Node-RED or on the Tasmota device itself.
Tenth, devices talk pub/sub over MQTT instead of point-to-point.
Eleventh, no batteries unless they last a year or more between changes.

I get asked about my Tasmota sprinkler controller the most, as well as the garage door setup. Both have been running for years with no real changes.

**What Readers Are Telling Me**

I shared this post on LinkedIn and Hacker News and asked for the most pointless internet dependency people had found in a device. Here are summarizations of those thoughts.

First, everyone has one. Most examples were basic functions locked behind an app, an account, or a server:
A fridge whose temperature can't be changed because of a bug in the manufacturer's phone app.
A TV that can't even switch inputs when the internet is down, and another that has to be signed in before it works as a plain HDMI monitor. One reader's older smart TV has dead apps but still works as a regular TV, which is how a device should fail.
A heating system whose app doesn't work from the local network.
A kitchen vent and a dishwasher that need the internet even when you control them from Home Assistant.
A smart plug that can't be set up on an isolated network.
Noise-cancelling headphones that search for a Bluetooth device forever, so you can't just turn on the noise cancelling.
Synthesizers and MIDI keyboards that need a cloud login for first use or firmware updates.
A meat thermometer that only worked through its app and stopped working when the company went out of business.

The meat thermometer bothers me most. The hardware outlived the company. It stopped working anyway.

Second, rules readers added:
Offline first, with the internet for extras. A device should do its main job on its own.
Treat every third-party dependency as a risk. APIs change, so plan a local fallback or a clear degraded mode. One mobile developer works the same way: the core action works offline or from cache, and only the extras wait for a connection.
Decide what must work offline. My answer is turning things on and off, temperature and safety controls, and anything a guest has to use without learning an app. As one reader said, if a visitor can't turn something on or adjust the temperature without an app account, the design has failed.
A stricter version of my first two rules: everything core works with the Wi-Fi or the automation server shut down, and smart features never take away the dumb ones. The wall switch should control the light directly and let automation flip it. A switch that only sends a request to the automation fails. That reader says my sprinkler controller fails this bar too. The only sprinkler controller they'd found with a local API and full control on the device itself was RainMachine, which is gone.
Clean up when you throw things away. Remove a connected printer from your account first. An abandoned connected device can be an easy way in for an attacker.

Third, practical tips:
Use local protocols. One reader recommends Zigbee and Z-Wave over Wi-Fi, because a switch that doesn't need the cloud tends to behave the same year after year. Another uses Zigbee for the smart plugs that can't be set up without internet.
Let an LLM find the firmware. One reader buys the cheapest hardware that does the job, ignores reviews that complain about the app, and asks an LLM for local-first custom firmware once it arrives. That has worked for their switches, a thermostat, an air quality monitor, a thermometer, and a window fan. They'd have paid about 2.5 times more for hardware that was local out of the box.
Old Nest thermostats have a way out. You can find open source firmware for Nest Gen 1 and Gen 2 thermostats at a site called No Longer Evil.
Smart TVs can be tamed. Reset the TV and never connect it to the network, and the input problem goes away.
Remote access is the cost of staying local. I use Tailscale on the router and keep the individual devices off the tailnet. One reader ran OpenVPN on OpenWrt, which worked but felt clunky, and is now trying Tailscale.
There's a gap to fill. One reader would happily pay for a pre-built, open "works with everything" Raspberry Pi image with MQTT. DietPi and OpenWrt come close and have a small footprint, but you need some understanding to get going. Another wanted a hub that ships with the house, like a water heater or a router. Home Assistant sells hub hardware that's close to that.
Right to repair is the bigger fight. Readers pointed to the right-to-repair movement and groups like the FULU Foundation, which work to keep companies from locking you out of what you bought.

Finally, counterpoints. Internet connectivity has real cases that are obvious, such as a meat thermometer that lets you watch the game while the steak cooks. Also, remote access to systems where you really need them, such as cameras.

**Devices I've Used**

These are the parts from my home automation builds, grouped by project. You can find the specific links on the blog post if you want to buy them.

For the sprinkler controller, I used a HiLetgo ESP8266 NodeMCU board, which is a Wi-Fi microcontroller that runs Tasmota. I used an SSD1306 OLED screen for a status display for the sprinkler state. An Inkbird SSR-40DA solid state relay switches the 24 VAC sprinkler valve. A BME280 sensor handles temperature, humidity, and pressure. And a 24 VAC power supply powers the sprinkler valve.

For lights, switches, and plugs, I used a Martin Jerry smart dimmer switch, which is a wall light switch that runs Tasmota out of the box. A Martin Jerry humidity fan switch runs the bathroom fan when humidity gets too high. A Sonoff S31 smart plug acts as a power watchdog for the cable modem. And a serial-to-USB adapter connects an ESP8266 to your PC for flashing Tasmota.

For the garage door, I used a Raspberry Pi 3, which runs Docker and the web API. A SunFounder 2-channel 5V relay module simulates the button press to toggle the door. And a LiftMaster wired control panel bypasses the anti-tamper wiring.

Around the house, I used a GL.iNet Flint 2 router, which runs OpenWrt and an MQTT broker locally. A cheap tablet is mounted on the wall as the dashboard. And a Lefant robot vacuum cleans the floors.
