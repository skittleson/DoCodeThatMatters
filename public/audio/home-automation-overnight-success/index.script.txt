A quick note before we dive in: I wrote the first draft of this, AI helped me edit and rewrite it, and I made the final pass. But the rules, setups, and talks are my own.

Here is the short version. Nothing core in my house depends on a vendor's server. If the internet goes down, the house keeps running. That has been rule one for about ten years, and my other rules came out of it. In this post, I’ll share those rules, and then walk you through both talks I’ve given on home automation, slide by slide.

### Why Local

My Nest integration worked until Google broke the API. That is the failure I plan around now. The talks have a few more examples: a Samsung fridge showing a TLS certificate error on its own screen, and a Camp Chef grill controller that needs Wi-Fi to show you the temperature.

Everything that matters in my house runs on hardware I own. That means Tasmota on ESP boards, an MQTT broker, and Node-RED on a Raspberry Pi. I do use some cloud services, mostly Alexa and a few AWS ones. But Alexa is just another way to flip a switch. If it’s down, the wall switches and the wall dashboard still work.

### The Rules

Here are the rules from the first talk, which were restated in the second.

First, no internet, no problem. Core stuff has to work with the internet unplugged. Alexa can be a way in, but never the only way.
Second, don’t remove the old way while adding the new one. If someone tapes a wall switch on so a smart bulb keeps power, I skipped this rule.
Third, be agile. Rooms get used differently and people move in and out, so it all has to be cheap to change.
Fourth, check your automations. I once had four scheduled triggers hitting the same garden pump.
Fifth, exceptions must be exceptional. I can break a rule, but only for a reason I’d still agree with six months later.

I added these next five rules after year five, once more people lived in the house.

Sixth, a cloud device needs an API with logs or it doesn’t get installed. I don’t want a black box in the house.
Seventh, config lives in one place instead of five vendor apps.
Eighth, there must be more than one way in: an app, Alexa, a QR code, or an NFC tag.
Ninth, if I keep doing the same steps, they become a routine.
Tenth, devices talk pub/sub over MQTT instead of point to point.
Eleventh, no batteries unless they last a year or more between changes. Nobody wants to swap a door sensor battery at 2 a.m.

The build I get asked about most is the Tasmota sprinkler controller. You can find the link on the blog post for the full details. It’s in both talks, and so is the garage door setup, which has been running about as long.

### Talk One: Home Automation

Nobody needs any of this. I like building things that just work in the background, and each automation is a small puzzle.

The next slide is titled "Connected Everything." It’s a collage of icons showing the modern mess where everything is online. Everything talks to a cloud now: phones, laptops, thermostats, locks. Those clouds may or may not be up when you need them, so know what you're signing up for before you plug something in.

Most people start here. The slide shows a Nest thermostat, an Amazon smart plug, and a Yale smart lock keypad. Each of these has its own app and account.

Every device lands somewhere on this triangle: Privacy, Integration and Flexibility, and Cost. You have to pick your tradeoffs. A cloud camera is cheap and easy, and it costs you privacy. A switch I flash myself takes longer to set up, but I can hook it into anything. I figure out where a device lands before I buy it.

This next image shows a light switch taped on with a note reading "Leave Switch On, This is BAD." Someone, often me, automates a light, then has to tape the wall switch on so the smart bulb keeps power. If a switch in a room ever did nothing, this is why.

### What Readers Told Me

I shared the "no internet, no problem" rule on LinkedIn and asked for the most pointless internet dependency people had found in a device. Dozens of people answered. A Hacker News thread added more. Here's what came back.

First, everyone has one. Most examples were basic functions locked behind an app, an account, or a server:
A fridge whose temperature can't be changed because of a bug in the manufacturer's phone app.
A TV that can't even switch inputs when the internet is down, and another that has to be signed in before it works as a plain HDMI monitor. One reader's older smart TV has dead apps but still works as a regular TV, which is how a device should fail.
A heating system whose app doesn't work from the local network.
A kitchen vent and a dishwasher that need the internet even when you control them from Home Assistant.
A smart plug that can't be set up on an isolated network.
Noise-cancelling headphones that search for a Bluetooth device forever, so you can't just turn on the noise cancelling.
Synthesizers and MIDI keyboards that need a cloud login for first use or firmware updates.
And a meat thermometer that only worked through its app and stopped working when the company went out of business.

The meat thermometer bothers me most. The hardware outlived the company, and it stopped working anyway.

Next, here are the rules readers added:
Offline first, with the internet for extras. A device should do its main job on its own.
Treat every third-party dependency as a risk. APIs change, so plan a local fallback or a clear degraded mode. One mobile developer works the same way: the core action works offline or from cache, and only the extras wait for a connection.
Decide what must work offline. My answer is turning things on and off, temperature and safety controls, and anything a guest has to use without learning an app. As one reader said, if a visitor can't turn something on or adjust the temperature without an app account, the design has failed.
A stricter version of my first two rules: everything core works with the Wi-Fi or the automation server shut down, and smart features never take away the dumb ones. The wall switch should control the light directly and let automation flip it. A switch that only sends a request to the automation fails. That reader says my sprinkler controller fails this bar too. The only sprinkler controller they'd found with a local API and full control on the device itself was RainMachine, which is gone.
And clean up when you throw things away. Remove a connected printer from your account first. An abandoned connected device can be an easy way in for an attacker.

Here are some practical tips:
Use local protocols. One reader recommends Zigbee and Z-Wave over Wi-Fi, because a switch that doesn't need the cloud tends to behave the same year after year. Another uses Zigbee for the smart plugs that can't be set up without internet.
Let an LLM find the firmware. One reader buys the cheapest hardware that does the job, ignores reviews that complain about the app, and asks an LLM for local-first custom firmware once it arrives. That has worked for their switches, a thermostat, an air quality monitor, a thermometer, and a window fan. They'd have paid about 2.5 times more for hardware that was local out of the box.
Old Nest thermostats have a way out. There is open source firmware called "No Longer Evil" for Nest Gen 1 and Gen 2 thermostats. You can find the link on the blog post.
Smart TVs can be tamed. Reset the TV and never connect it to the network, and the input problem goes away.
Remote access is the cost of staying local. I use Tailscale on the router and keep the individual devices off the tailnet. One reader ran OpenVPN on OpenWrt, which worked but felt clunky, and is now trying Tailscale.
There's a gap to fill. One reader would happily pay for a pre-built, open "works with everything" Raspberry Pi image with MQTT. DietPi and OpenWrt come close and have a small footprint, but you need some understanding to get going. Another wanted a hub that ships with the house, like a water heater or a router. Home Assistant sells hub hardware that's close to that.
Right to repair is the bigger fight. Readers pointed to the right-to-repair movement and groups like the FULU Foundation, which work to keep companies from locking you out of what you bought. You can find the link on the blog post.

Finally, some counterpoints. Connectivity has real uses. A connected meat thermometer lets you watch the game while the steak cooks. The same reader also had an oven decide to update its software in the middle of cooking. One reader pointed out the business model behind all of this: companies would rather sell you a service than a product. Another expects the shift away from internet-connected devices to come from resilience worries rather than consumer complaints, since enough connected homes make a target. And one skips connected appliances entirely, keeping only computers and phones online.
