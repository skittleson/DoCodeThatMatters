Rule one is that nothing in my house needs a vendor's server to work. The rest of the rules I follow after ten years of home automation, plus both talks I gave on it, slide by slide.

I wrote the first draft, AI helped me edit and rewrite it, and I made the final pass. The rules, setups, and talks are my own.

The short version is this: nothing core in my house depends on a vendor's server. If the internet goes down, the house keeps running. That's been rule one for about ten years, and my other rules came out of it. This post has the rules, then both talks I've given on home automation, slide by slide.

Why local?

My Nest integration worked until Google broke the API. That's the failure I plan around now. The talks have a few more: a Samsung fridge showing a TLS certificate error on its own screen, and a Camp Chef grill controller that needs Wi-Fi to show you the temperature.

Everything that matters runs on hardware I own. That's Tasmota on ESP boards, an MQTT broker, and Node-RED on a Raspberry Pi. I do use some cloud services, mostly Alexa and a few AWS ones. Alexa is just another way to flip a switch. If it's down, the wall switches and the wall dashboard still work.

The rules

From the first talk, restated in the second:

First, no internet, no problem. Core stuff has to work with the internet unplugged. Alexa can be a way in, but never the only way.

Second, don't remove the old way while adding the new one. If someone tapes a wall switch on so a smart bulb keeps power, I skipped this rule.

Third, be agile. Rooms get used differently and people move in and out, so it all has to be cheap to change.

Fourth, check your automations. I once had four scheduled triggers hitting the same garden pump.

Fifth, exceptions must be exceptional. I can break a rule, but only for a reason I'd still agree with six months later.

Added after year five, once more people lived in the house:

Sixth, a cloud device needs an API with logs or it doesn't get installed. I don't want a black box in the house.

Seventh, config lives in one place instead of five vendor apps.

Eighth, more than one way in: an app, Alexa, a QR code, or an NFC tag.

Ninth, if I keep doing the same steps, they become a routine.

Tenth, devices talk pub/sub over MQTT instead of point to point.

Eleventh, no batteries unless they last a year or more between changes. Nobody wants to swap a door sensor battery at 2am.

The build I get asked about most is the Tasmota sprinkler controller. It's in both talks, and so is the garage door setup, which has been running about as long.

Talk One: Home Automation

First up, the title slide: Home Automation, for fun and convenience.

Nobody needs any of this. I do it because I like building things that work in the background, and every automation is a small puzzle with a real payoff when it's solved.

Next is the "Connected Everything" collage of icons. This is the backdrop for the whole talk. Phones, laptops, tablets, thermostats, and locks all talk to clouds that may or may not be up when you need them. Connectivity is fine. Just know what you're opting into before you plug it in.

Then we see the retail starting point: a Nest thermostat, an Amazon smart plug, and a Yale smart lock keypad. Most people start here with a thermostat, a plug, and a lock, and that's a fine place to start. Each one ships with its own app and its own account.

Next is the "Privacy, Integration/Flexibility, and Cost" triangle. Every device you add lands somewhere on this triangle. A cloud camera is cheap and convenient, and you pay for it with privacy. A switch I flash myself takes more setup time, and in return I can integrate it with anything. I figure out where a device lands before it goes in the cart.

Then there's an image of a light switch taped on with a note reading "Leave Switch On, This is BAD". Someone, often me, automates a light and then has to tape the wall switch on so the smart bulb always has power. If you've ever walked into a room and the switch did nothing, this is why.

The better fix for the taped switch is an in-wall Wi-Fi switch like the Wemo one shown in the next slide. The wall switch keeps working the normal way and I still get a hook to automate. It takes more work to install than a smart plug, but nobody has to retrain their hand.

Then comes "No Internet, No Problem," with scissors cutting an ethernet cable. Rule one, and the one I don't bend on. If the internet goes down, my lights still turn on.

Next is "Must Be Agile," showing sticky notes on a planning board. Home automation is never done. Rooms get used differently, people move in and out, and new devices show up. Whatever you build has to be cheap to change.

Then we see a Node-RED flow for Garage Cool Off logic. The garage cooling flow starts from a manual trigger and checks whether the door is closed. From there it opens the door, waits, flips the fan on, or sends a notification if cooling is already running. It's a small flow and it has kept the garage from baking more than once.

The next slide shows the Node-RED garage sensor flow feeding Grafana, plus an "Opens" counter and "On Status" chart. The door sensor and reed switch both report into a transform step that sets a global garage-closed flag and logs to Grafana. Below that is the chart from a few real days: 21 opens, with spikes showing when each one happened. It's easy to skip reporting or alerting. This can alert me if the garage was left open, which is useful when I accidentally leave it open.

Next, we see Node-RED scheduled injects feeding a Garden Water Pump node. Four scheduled triggers all point at the same pump node. It's easy to end up with five automations fighting over one device. Every so often I look at the flow and ask whether they still make sense together.

Then there's the Captain Jack Sparrow meme: "The Rules, more of what you'd call guidelines than actual rules." I've broken every one of these rules plenty of times. They're guidelines.

Next is a slide showing the Node-RED logo, a Phillips Hue IFTTT-style applet, and an Alexa routine editor. Here are three ways to say "event in, action out." Node-RED is my main tool. Alexa routines and IFTTT-style community applets cover the cases where I don't want to build something from scratch.

Then we see an MQTT publish/subscribe diagram with a temperature sensor, broker, and two subscribers. MQTT is what keeps the pieces decoupled. The temperature sensor publishes and doesn't care who's listening. A phone and a backend system can both subscribe to the same topic, and the sensor needs no extra logic. The sprinkler controller in the Tasmota post is built on the same pattern.

Next is a Sonoff Mini Wi-Fi DIY smart switch and a Node-RED fireplace flow. The Sonoff Mini is small enough to hide inside the fireplace's existing wiring. The flow lets either the dashboard or Alexa flip it. It makes the "memorable automations" list later because a fake fire you can turn on by voice makes a good party trick.

Then we see the sprinkler controller wiring, a 3D PCB render, the Tasmota web UI, scheduled nodes, and a bare PCB layout. The whole sprinkler build: the relay and ESP module in the enclosure, the 3D board render, the Tasmota UI with BME280 sensor readings, the Node-RED schedule feeding the pump, and the finished PCB layout. The sprinkler post has the full writeup, including why I picked Tasmota over custom firmware. It's a bit hacky but I love it.

Next is the "Rules Guidelines Recap" bullet list. The recap slide is the whole talk in five lines. I break the first one, don't introduce a new way while removing the old way, carelessly more often than the others, and I regret it every time. The last one keeps the other four honest. "I was in a hurry" doesn't count as exceptional.

Then we see the "Memorable Home Automations" bullet list. This is a grab bag: heart-rate light blinking, fireplace sim, rain-skip watering, phone-presence garage, AWS services, and 3D printer status lights.

Finally, the closing slide for talk one: "Questions? Feedback!"

That was talk one. Almost everything on the "memorable automations" slide later picked up a new failure mode or got replaced, which is why there's a sequel.

What readers told me

I shared the "no internet, no problem" rule on LinkedIn and asked for the most pointless internet dependency people had found in a device. Dozens of people answered. A Hacker News thread added more. Here's what came back.

Everyone has one

Most examples were basic functions locked behind an app, an account, or a server:

First, a fridge whose temperature can't be changed because of a bug in the manufacturer's phone app.

Second, a TV that can't even switch inputs when the internet is down, and another that has to be signed in before it works as a plain HDMI monitor. One reader's older smart TV has dead apps but still works as a regular TV, which is how a device should fail.

Third, a heating system whose app doesn't work from the local network.

Fourth, a kitchen vent and a dishwasher that need the internet even when you control them from Home Assistant.

Fifth, a smart plug that can't be set up on an isolated network.

Sixth, noise-cancelling headphones that search for a Bluetooth device forever, so you can't just turn on the noise cancelling.

Seventh, synthesizers and MIDI keyboards that need a cloud login for first use or firmware updates.

Eighth, a meat thermometer that only worked through its app and stopped working when the company went out of business.

The meat thermometer bothers me most. The hardware outlived the company, and it stopped working anyway.

Rules readers added

First, offline first, with the internet for extras. A device should do its main job on its own.

Second, treat every third-party dependency as a risk. APIs change, so plan a local fallback or a clear degraded mode. One mobile developer works the same way: the core action works offline or from cache, and only the extras wait for a connection.

Third, decide what must work offline. My answer is turning things on and off, temperature and safety controls, and anything a guest has to use without learning an app. As one reader said, if a visitor can't turn something on or adjust the temperature without an app account, the design has failed.

Fourth, a stricter version of my first two rules: everything core works with the Wi-Fi or the automation server shut down, and smart features never take away the dumb ones. The wall switch should control the light directly and let automation flip it. A switch that only sends a request to the automation fails. That reader says my sprinkler controller fails this bar too. The only sprinkler controller they'd found with a local API and full control on the device itself was RainMachine, which is gone.

Fifth, clean up when you throw things away. Remove a connected printer from your account first. An abandoned connected device can be an easy way in for an attacker.

Practical tips

First, use local protocols. One reader recommends Zigbee and Z-Wave over Wi-Fi, because a switch that doesn't need the cloud tends to behave the same year after year. Another uses Zigbee for the smart plugs that can't be set up without internet.

Second, let an LLM find the firmware. One reader buys the cheapest hardware that does the job, ignores reviews that complain about the app, and asks an LLM for local-first custom firmware once it arrives. That has worked for their switches, a thermostat, an air quality monitor, a thermometer, and a window fan. They'd have paid about 2.5x for hardware that was local out of the box.

Third, old Nest thermostats have a way out. You can find the link to No Longer Evil on the blog post. It is open source firmware for Nest Gen 1 and Gen 2 thermostats.

Fourth, smart TVs can be tamed. Reset the TV and never connect it to the network, and the input problem goes away.

Fifth, remote access is the cost of staying local. I use Tailscale on the router and keep the individual devices off the tailnet. One reader ran OpenVPN on OpenWrt, which worked but felt clunky, and is now trying Tailscale.

Sixth, there's a gap to fill. One reader would happily pay for a pre-built, open "works with everything" Raspberry Pi image with MQTT. DietPi and OpenWrt come close and have a small footprint, but you need some understanding to get going. Another wanted a hub that ships with the house, like a water heater or a router. Home Assistant sells hub hardware that's close to that.

Seventh, right to repair is the bigger fight. Readers pointed to the right-to-repair movement and groups like the FULU Foundation, which work to keep companies from locking you out of what you bought. You can find the link to the FULU Foundation on the blog post.

Counterpoints

Connectivity has real uses. A connected meat thermometer lets you watch the game while the steak cooks. The same reader also had an oven decide to update its software in the middle of cooking. One reader pointed out the business model behind all of this: companies would rather sell you a service than a product. Another expects the shift away from internet-connected devices to come from resilience worries rather than consumer complaints, since enough connected homes make a target. And one skips connected appliances entirely, keeping only computers and phones online.
