So, here is a quick note before we dive in. I wrote the first draft of this, some AI helped me edit and rewrite it, and then I made the final pass myself. But the rules, the setups, and the talks? Those are all mine.

Here is the short version. Every core smart device in my house does not depend on a vendor’s server. If the internet goes down, everything still works, though maybe with a downgraded experience. This has been my rule for ten years, and honestly, it has been challenging to follow. I’ve given two talks about it, and I’m going to walk you through each one, slide by slide.

Let’s start with why I care so much about keeping things local. My Nest integration worked one day, and then it just didn’t. They had changed the API, around the same time Google acquired them. I have a few more examples in these talks that show a Samsung fridge and a grill controller that needs Wi-Fi just to show you the temperature. I don’t need, and I shouldn’t need, Wi-Fi or a vendor service to interact with hardware.

So, everything that matters runs on hardware I own. Mostly on Tasmota running on ESP8266 or ESP32 boards, an MQTT broker, and Node-RED on a Raspberry Pi. I do use some cloud services for enhancements, like Alexa and a few AWS tools, but those are secondary. If the internet is down, the switches all work like normal.

Let’s talk about the rules. From my first talk, here is the original list. First, no internet, no problem. Core stuff has to work with the internet unplugged. Second, don’t remove the old way while adding the new one. Third, be agile. Fourth, check your automations. Fifth, exceptions must be exceptional.

Then, after year five, once more people lived in the house, I added six more. Sixth, a cloud device needs an API with logs or it doesn’t get installed. I don’t want a black box in the house. Seventh, configuration lives in one place instead of five vendor apps. Eighth, there should be more than one way to do something, whether that’s an app, Alexa, a QR code, or an NFC tag. Ninth, if I keep doing the same steps, it becomes routine, either in Node-RED or on the Tasmota device itself. Tenth, devices talk pub/sub over MQTT instead of point to point. And eleventh, no batteries unless they last a year or more between changes.

I get asked about the Tasmota sprinkler controller the most, as well as the garage door setup. You can find the link to that specific post on the blog if you want to see it. Both have been running for years with no real changes.

So, what did readers tell me? I shared the "no internet, no problem" rule on LinkedIn and asked for the most pointless internet dependency people had found in a device. Dozens of people answered. A Hacker News thread added more.

First, everyone has one. Most examples were basic functions locked behind an app, an account, or a server. For example, a fridge whose temperature can't be changed because of a bug in the manufacturer's phone app. Or a TV that can't even switch inputs when the internet is down, and another that has to be signed in before it works as a plain HDMI monitor. One reader's older smart TV has dead apps but still works as a regular TV, which is how a device should fail.

There was also a heating system whose app doesn't work from the local network. A kitchen vent and a dishwasher that need the internet even when you control them from Home Assistant. A smart plug that can't be set up on an isolated network. Noise-cancelling headphones that search for a Bluetooth device forever, so you can't just turn on the noise cancelling. Synthesizers and MIDI keyboards that need a cloud login for first use or firmware updates. And finally, a meat thermometer that only worked through its app and stopped working when the company went out of business. The meat thermometer bothers me most. The hardware outlived the company. It stopped working anyway.

Readers also added some rules. First, offline first, with the internet for extras. A device should do its main job on its own. Second, treat every third-party dependency as a risk. APIs change, so plan a local fallback or a clear degraded mode. One mobile developer works the same way: the core action works offline or from cache, and only the extras wait for a connection.

Third, decide what must work offline. My answer is turning things on and off, temperature and safety controls, and anything a guest has to use without learning an app. As one reader said, if a visitor can't turn something on or adjust the temperature without an app account, the design has failed. Fourth, a stricter version of my first two rules: everything core works with the Wi-Fi or the automation server shut down, and smart features never take away the dumb ones. The wall switch should control the light directly and let automation flip it. A switch that only sends a request to the automation fails. That reader says my sprinkler controller fails this bar too. The only sprinkler controller they'd found with a local API and full control on the device itself was RainMachine, which is gone.

Fifth, clean up when you throw things away. Remove a connected printer from your account first. An abandoned connected device can be an easy way in for an attacker.

They also shared some practical tips. First, use local protocols. One reader recommends Zigbee and Z-Wave over Wi-Fi, because a switch that doesn't need the cloud tends to behave the same year after year. Another uses Zigbee for the smart plugs that can't be set up without internet. Second, let an LLM find the firmware. One reader buys the cheapest hardware that does the job, ignores reviews that complain about the app, and asks an LLM for local-first custom firmware once it arrives. That has worked for their switches, a thermostat, an air quality monitor, a thermometer, and a window fan. They'd have paid about 2.5x for hardware that was local out of the box.

Third, old Nest thermostats have a way out. There is open source firmware called "No Longer Evil" for Nest Gen 1 and Gen 2 thermostats; you can find it on their website. Fourth, smart TVs can be tamed. Reset the TV and never connect it to the network, and the input problem goes away.

Fifth, remote access is the cost of staying local. I use Tailscale on the router and keep the individual devices off the tailnet. One reader ran OpenVPN on OpenWrt, which worked but felt clunky, and is now trying Tailscale. Sixth, there's a gap to fill. One reader would happily pay for a pre-built, open "works with everything" Raspberry Pi image with MQTT. DietPi and OpenWrt come close and have a small footprint, but you need some understanding to get going. Another wanted a hub that ships with the house, like a water heater or a router. Home Assistant sells hub hardware that's close to that.

Finally, right to repair is the bigger fight. Readers pointed to the right-to-repair movement and groups like the FULU Foundation, which work to keep companies from locking you out of what you bought.

There were also some counterpoints. Connectivity has real uses. A connected meat thermometer lets you watch the game while the steak cooks. The same reader also had an oven decide to update its software in the middle of cooking. One reader pointed out the business model behind all of this: companies would rather sell you a service than a product. Another expects the shift away from internet-connected devices to come from resilience worries rather than consumer complaints, since enough connected homes make a target. And one skips connected appliances entirely, keeping only computers and phones online.
