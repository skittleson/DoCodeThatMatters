Hey everyone. Let’s talk about smart home devices, and specifically, why they shouldn’t need the internet to work.

Before we dive in, a quick note: I wrote the first draft of this, AI helped me edit and rewrite it, and I made the final pass. The rules, setups, and talks described here are my own. Also, for those who might be skeptical of AI-written content, I’ve reworked most of this post to be written in my own voice. The slide decks are probably the most interesting part, and LLMs were used to add context there. You can find links to download the first and second talk PDFs on the blog post itself.

Here is the short version: Every core smart device in my house does not depend on a vendor’s server. If the internet goes down, everything still works, though with a downgraded experience. It has been my rule for ten years, and it has been challenging to follow. I’ve given two talks about it, and we are going to go through them slide by slide.

**Why Local**

Let me start with why this matters. My Nest integration worked one day, and then it didn’t. They had changed the API around the same time Google acquired them. I have a few more examples in these talks, like a Samsung fridge and a grill controller that needs Wi-Fi just to show the temperature. I don’t need, and shouldn’t need, Wi-Fi or vendor services to interact with hardware.

So, everything that matters runs on hardware I own. Mostly on Tasmota running on ESP8266 or ESP32 boards, an MQTT broker, and Node-RED on a Raspberry Pi. I do use some cloud services for enhancements, like Alexa and a few AWS ones, but those are secondary. If the internet is down, the switches all work like normal.

**The Rules**

Let’s look at the rules from my first talk:

First, no internet, no problem. Core stuff has to work with the internet unplugged.
Second, don’t remove the old way while adding the new one.
Third, be agile.
Fourth, check your automations.
Fifth, exceptions must be exceptional.

I added a few more after year five, once more people lived in the house:

Sixth, a cloud device needs an API with logs or it doesn’t get installed. I don’t want a black box in the house.
Seventh, configuration lives in one place instead of five vendor apps.
Eighth, there should be more than one way to do something. An app, Alexa, a QR code, or an NFC tag.
Ninth, if I keep doing the same steps, it becomes routine, so I automate it in Node-RED or on the Tasmota device itself.
Tenth, devices talk via publish-subscribe over MQTT instead of point to point.
Eleventh, no batteries unless they last a year or more between changes.

I get asked about the Tasmota sprinkler controller and the garage door setup the most. You can find links to those specific posts on the blog. Both have been running for years with no real changes.

**Talk One: Home Automation**

Let’s start with the first talk. The title slide says, "Home Automation, for fun and convenience."

Nobody needs any of this. I like building things that just work in the background. Each automation is a small puzzle. It’s a hobby.

The next slide is a collage of icons titled "Connected Everything." It represents the modern mess where everything is online.

Everything seems to want to reach out to the cloud. Laptops and phones making sense, sure. However, my thermostats and locks? Smart devices need to be questioned since they could be critically useful in your home. Companies may go out of business, so be mindful of what you’re signing up for.

The next image shows a Nest thermostat, an Amazon smart plug, and a Yale smart lock keypad. This is the retail starting point: thermostat, smart plug, smart lock. Each can have its own account and app. Usually, people try to combine devices into the same ecosystem when possible.

Here’s a triangle graphic: Privacy, Integration and Flexibility, and Cost. You pick your tradeoffs. A cloud camera is cheap and easy, but in exchange for costs, you give up privacy. A smart plug I flash myself takes longer to set up, but I can hook it into anything. Figure out where a device lands on that triangle before buying it.

This next image shows a light switch taped to the wall with a note reading, "Leave Switch On. This is BAD." This is bad behavior. Don’t tape switches to the on position just so automation can take over.

The better fix is an in-wall Wi-Fi switch, like a Wemo. It still works like a normal switch, and I can automate it. It’s more work to install than a smart plug, but nobody has to change their habits.

Here is the title slide for Rule One: "No Internet, No Problem." It shows scissors cutting an ethernet cable. The message is clear: if the internet goes down, my lights still turn on.

Next up: "Must Be Agile," with a picture of sticky notes on a planning board. Being agile means being willing to understand that changes will come to your system.

This next slide shows a Node-RED flow for "Garage Cool Off" logic. It checks if the door is closed before running the fan. This is a small workflow to keep my garage cool in the summer. I’ve had to change it multiple times.

The following image shows that same garage sensor flow feeding into Grafana, plus an "Opens Counter" and "On Status" chart. I have a reed switch wired up to the Tasmota ESP32 that controls the garage door. I use Grafana to monitor the state changes. I want to know if the garage door was left open so I can get a message that it’s been open for too long.

Next is a Node-RED flow showing scheduled injects feeding a Garden Water Pump node. These are scheduled triggers on a single pump. Be careful here; automations fighting over one device can happen, and it has caused me problems. Make it a habit to check every so often.

This slide features a Captain Jack Sparrow meme: "The Rules... more of what you’d call guidelines than actual rules." I’ve broken many of these rules, so now I refer to them as guidelines.

Here we see three tools: the Node-RED logo, a Philips Hue IFTTT-style applet, and an Alexa routine editor. These are three ways to do "event in, action out." Node-RED is my main tool. Alexa routines and IFTTT-style applets cover the stuff I don’t want to build myself.

The next image is an MQTT publish-subscribe diagram with a temperature sensor, a broker, and two subscribers. MQTT keeps things decoupled as a pub-sub event system. The temperature sensor publishes and doesn’t care who’s listening. So, a phone and a backend can both subscribe without the sensor doing anything extra. The sprinkler controller I mentioned earlier works the same way.

Here is a Sonoff Mini Wi-Fi DIY smart switch and a Node-RED fireplace flow. A Sonoff Mini fits inside the fireplace wiring, and the dashboard or Alexa can flip it. A fake fire you can turn on by voice is a good party trick.

This collage shows the sprinkler build end-to-end: the relay and ESP module in the enclosure, the 3D board render, the Tasmota UI with BME280 sensor readings, the Node-RED schedule feeding the pump, and the finished PCB layout. The full writeup is on the blog post I linked earlier, including why I picked Tasmota over custom firmware. It’s a bit hacky, but I love it.

This slide recaps the rules in five lines: don’t swap the new way in before the old way is gone, no internet no problem, must be agile, check automations, exceptions must be exceptional. The one I break most is the first, don’t introduce a new way while removing the old one, and I regret it every time. The last one stops me from breaking the others for no good reason. "I was in a hurry" doesn’t count.

The next slide is a grab bag of memorable automations: heart-rate light blinking, fireplace simulation, rain-skip watering, phone-presence garage, AWS services, and 3D printer status lights.

And that’s talk one, ending with a slide asking for questions and feedback. Most of the "memorable automations" later broke in some new way or got replaced, so there’s a sequel.

The next slide asks, "What has changed? That’s why I’m here today." The rules stayed the same, but more were added.

**What Readers are telling me**

I shared this post on LinkedIn and asked for the most pointless internet dependency people had found in a device. Dozens of people answered, then Hacker News commenters added more.

**Everyone has one**

Most examples were basic functions locked behind an app, an account, or a server:

First, a fridge whose temperature can’t be changed because of a bug in the manufacturer’s phone app.
Second, a TV that can’t even switch inputs when the internet is down, and another that has to be signed in before it works as a plain HDMI monitor. One reader’s older smart TV has dead apps but still works as a regular TV, which is how a device should fail.
Third, a heating system whose app doesn’t work from the local network.
Fourth, a kitchen vent and a dishwasher that need the internet even when you control them from Home Assistant.
Fifth, a smart plug that can’t be set up on an isolated network.
Sixth, noise-cancelling headphones that search for a Bluetooth device forever, so you can’t just turn on the noise cancelling.
Seventh, synthesizers and MIDI keyboards that need a cloud login for first use or firmware updates.
And eighth, a meat thermometer that only worked through its app and stopped working when the company went out of business.

The meat thermometer bothers me most. The hardware outlived the company, but it stopped working anyway.

**Rules readers added**

First, offline first, with the internet for extras. A device should do its main job on its own.
Second, treat every third-party dependency as a risk. APIs change, so plan a local fallback or a clear degraded mode. One mobile developer works the same way: the core action works offline or from cache, and only the extras wait for a connection.
Third, decide what must work offline. My answer is turning things on and off, temperature and safety controls, and anything a guest has to use without learning an app. As one reader said, if a visitor can’t turn something on or adjust the temperature without an app account, the design has failed.
Fourth, a stricter version of my first two rules: everything core works with the Wi-Fi or the automation server shut down, and smart features never take away the dumb ones. The wall switch should control the light directly and let automation flip it. A switch that only sends a request to the automation fails. That reader says my sprinkler controller fails this bar too. The only sprinkler controller they’d found with a local API and full control on the device itself was RainMachine, which is gone.
Fifth, clean up when you throw things away. Remove a connected printer from your account first. An abandoned connected device can be an easy way in for an attacker.

**Practical tips**

First, use local protocols. One reader recommends Zigbee and Z-Wave over Wi-Fi, because a switch that doesn’t need the cloud tends to behave the same year after year. Another uses Zigbee for the smart plugs that can’t be set up without internet.
Second, let an LLM find the firmware. One reader buys the cheapest hardware that does the job, ignores reviews that complain about the app, and asks an LLM for local-first custom firmware once it arrives. That has worked for their switches, a thermostat, an air quality monitor, a thermometer, and a window fan. They’d have paid about two and a half times more for hardware that was local out of the box.
Third, old Nest thermostats have a way out. There is open source firmware called "No Longer Evil" for Nest Gen 1 and Gen 2 thermostats. You can find the link on the blog post.
Fourth, smart TVs can be tamed. Reset the TV and never connect it to the network, and the input problem goes away.
Fifth, remote access is the cost of staying local. I use Tailscale on the router and keep the individual devices off the tailnet. One reader ran OpenVPN on OpenWrt, which worked but felt clunky, and is now trying Tailscale.
Sixth, there’s a gap to fill. One reader would happily pay for a pre-built, open "works with everything" Raspberry Pi image with MQTT. DietPi and OpenWrt come close and have a small footprint, but you need some understanding to get going. Another wanted a hub that ships with the house, like a water heater or a router. Home Assistant sells hub hardware that’s close to that.
Seventh, right to repair is the bigger fight. Readers pointed to the right-to-repair movement and groups like the FULU Foundation, which work to keep companies from locking you out of what you bought. You can find the link to them on the blog post.

**Counterpoints**

Connectivity has real uses. A connected meat thermometer lets you watch the game while the steak cooks. The same reader also had an oven decide to update its software in the middle of cooking. One reader pointed out the business model behind all of this: companies would rather sell you a service than a product. Another expects the shift away from internet-connected devices to come from resilience worries rather than consumer complaints, since enough connected homes make a target. And one skips connected appliances entirely, keeping only computers and phones online.
