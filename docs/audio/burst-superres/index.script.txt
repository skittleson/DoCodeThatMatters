Out of eight thousand, eight hundred and nine Sony photos in my Immich library, exactly one burst was steady enough to merge cleanly. That one number changed what the app had to be. Burst Super-Res is free, open source under the MIT license, runs offline on macOS, Windows, and Linux, and you can find the download link on the blog post.

If you have a Sony A6000 or A6400, this is part of a larger series. You can find links to Part 1 on modernization, Part 2 on more hacks, Part 3 on tethered Raspberry Pi setups, and various research resources right on the blog post.

There’s an image at the top of the post showing the app in action. It depicts a real eight-shot burst from a Sony A6000 taken in a canyon. There’s a slider you can imagine dragging; to the left of it is one photo upscaled by two times, and to the right is the average of all eight shots.

So, what is this? You drop in a burst of photos of the same still scene. The app aligns them to a fraction of a pixel, merges them, and gives you one image with twice the width and height. For example, four photos that are 6000 by 4000 pixels become one image that is 12000 by 8000. It reads JPEG, PNG, and Sony ARW raw files, keeps your EXIF data, and shows a split slider so you can see what the merge actually did.

There is no AI anywhere in the processing. Every output pixel comes from pixels your camera actually recorded, averaged after alignment. AI upscalers invent detail that looks plausible. This tool only uses detail that is really there, which is also why the gain is small.

What you get is less noise and cleaner fine edges. You do not get four times the detail, and the app never shows a megapixel number because of that. On simulated bursts with a known answer, the gain over one upscaled photo measured plus 0.7 to plus 1.3 decibels in PSNR, or Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio. That’s real, but modest.

Why did I build it? Part 1 of my Sony series has a super-resolution trick: shoot four photos, upscale each by 200 percent, align them, blend two at 50 percent, then blend the two results. On paper, that’s 97.2 megapixels. In practice, it looked more like 48.6 megapixels. It worked, but nobody was going to set up a Python environment for it. I wanted a free tool people could just download.

How did I get here? I set a few rules before writing anything. First, no AI in the processing; every pixel has to come from the photos. Second, measure on real data and publish the numbers, including where a feature hurts. Third, cross-platform without Electron. The app uses tinyjs, which runs the UI in the OS webview, so the Linux build is about 10 megabytes.

Then I leaned on research and tools, not intuition. Every idea below got measured against a benchmark before it went in.

The benchmark works like this: A real burst has no "true" high-resolution image to score against. So I made one. Take a real 24-megapixel A6000 or A6400 photo as the answer, shift copies by random sub-pixel amounts, downsample them by two times the way a sensor pixel averages light, add noise, save as JPEG, then merge them back and score against the original. The baseline is the same reference photo upscaled two times with Lanczos-3. Same output size, with and without the burst.

The early numbers were quietly wrong. The harness decoded with jpeg-js, which truncates instead of rounding. That shifted every pixel by minus 0.53 levels per round trip and skewed every score. Switching to libjpeg-turbo’s djpeg and cjpeg fixed it. Every number in this post comes from that harness, and you can run it yourself with a simple npm command found on the blog.

The original method is just a mean. The pairwise 50-50 blend from Part 1 works out to a plain average with extra rounding. For four photos, you blend A plus B, blend C plus D, then blend those two, so every photo ends up at 25 percent. That’s a mean, rounded to 8 bits after each stage. For three photos, it’s worse, because the weights come out as 25, 25, and 50.

So the default became a per-pixel mean, or median, over all aligned photos, with exact weights for any count. The pairwise mode is still in the app so you can compare against the original.

The bigger change was alignment. The Python version matched SIFT features and fit a homography. The new version uses sub-pixel registration instead: an image pyramid, Lucas-Kanade on tiles, then an affine fit through the tile shifts. On simulated bursts, it lands within 0.03 pixels, including bursts rotated by half a degree.

True shift-and-add got tried and dropped. It places every input pixel at its exact sub-pixel spot on the two times grid. On synthetic test patterns with heavy aliasing, it beat the mean by 1.0 to 1.7 decibels. On real photos, it tied the mean, and on a detailed landscape crop, it was 0.2 to 0.5 decibels worse. Out it went.

You need a tripod, don't forget it. The early version only had simulated bursts to show, so I went looking for real ones in Immich. I pulled metadata for every Sony photo: 1,880 from the A6400 and 6,929 from the A6000. Shots taken seconds apart with identical settings gave 732 burst candidates, and I ran the app's own alignment over 348 of them.

302 were handheld, more than 10 pixels off. Exactly one was steady: a 13-shot A6000 canyon burst. That’s the photo at the top of this post. Even that one had three frames that didn't line up well enough for the early version.

There is an image showing one photo upscaled two times next to five real photos merged. It’s a 200 percent crop. You can see less noise and fewer JPEG block edges in the merged version.

That’s 302 of 348 bursts shot handheld, from someone who wrote a guide on this. A tool that only works on a tripod is a tool for almost nobody.

Before building more, I looked at how comparable tools handle it. DeepSkyStacker scores every frame and stacks only the best ones; the early version just warned about bad frames and made you untick them by hand. Lightroom uses backslash to toggle before and after and Y for side by side; photographers already know those keys. Topaz Gigapixel compares multiple results with one shared navigator. Sony's own pixel-shift workflow tells you the shooting rules and the wait time up front. And Google’s paper on Handheld Multi-Frame Super-Resolution treats hand tremor as the source of the useful sub-pixel shifts, as long as each region gets aligned on its own.

That last one changed the plan. Version 0.2 added local alignment: when the global fit is worse than 0.1 pixel, a grid of 64-pixel cells gets measured and corrected on top of it. On a real handheld pair where the camera moved 52 pixels, and the near cactus and far hills shifted by different amounts, the error went from 4.36 pixels (a doubled cactus) to 0.06 pixels. All eight canyon frames came in at 0.22 pixels or better.

There’s an image showing the same handheld pair merged with one shift and rotation versus local alignment. On the left, you see a single shift and rotation for the whole photo, which is 4.36 pixels off. On the right, you see local alignment, which is only 0.06 pixels off.

Version 0.2 also picked up the Lightroom keys, Good, Soft, and Too far off badges per photo, automatic unticking of bad frames, and masking of 64-pixel areas where something moved (water, leaves, people) so they don't ghost. When a burst really is too far gone, the app says so instead of pretending.

There’s another image showing the app warning that a soft photo is used and part of it was left out. It shows two handheld shots four seconds apart, about 370 pixels of movement. The app uses the photo but says it's soft.

Next I went through the research on alignment and stacking, and measured the cheap ideas before adopting any of them. I tested a four-frame simulated burst. One photo upscaled two times scored 33.51 decibels on an A6000 landscape and 38.60 on an A6400 photo that was mostly out of focus. The mean merge scored 34.58 and 39.84 respectively. Mean plus gentle deblur scored 35.54 and 39.79. Sigma-clipped mean scored between 34.48 and 34.54, and 39.77 to 39.82. Deblur on one photo alone scored 32.69 and 37.56.

Deblurring only helps after the merge. That matches the two-step approach in Farsiu et al.'s paper: stacking lowers the noise enough for deconvolution to work. On a single photo, it made things worse. Sigma clipping, the astro stacking staple from Siril, didn't help because the per-area masking already handles moving things.

That turned into three features in version 0.3. First, sharpening after the merge. A gentle Richardson-Lucy deconvolution. "Auto" picks the amount by leaving one photo out of the merge and choosing whatever predicts that photo best. It's off by default for camera JPEGs, because they're already sharpened in camera and Auto measured up to 0.9 decibels worse on those. Second, brightness matching. Exposure flicker gets measured per photo and color evened out. With plus or minus 5 percent simulated flicker, it recovered the true gains to within 0.001. Third, RAW support for Sony ARW. Wronski's point is that a camera JPEG has already thrown away the fine aliasing detail super-resolution needs. So with six or more ARW files, every output pixel gets built straight from the raw sensor samples of all the photos, with no demosaicing in between. The ARW decoder is written from scratch in JavaScript and matches LibRaw on all 24.2 million samples of a real A6000 file. In simulations, RAW scored 0.9 to 2.5 decibels better than merging the same burst as JPEGs.

There is an image showing six camera-style JPEGs merged next to the same six RAW files merged from sensor samples. It’s a simulated six-photo burst.

I have exactly one real ARW to test with and no real RAW burst. So every RAW quality number above is from simulated bursts.

Deep-learning burst super-resolution tops the benchmarks, but it also invents detail and needs a big model or a GPU, which breaks the first rule. If you want to go deeper on the classical side, there are re-implementations of Google's method and papers on optical-flow alignment for scenes with a lot of depth; you can find those links on the blog post.

Where does the pixel work run? tinyjs apps have two halves: a txiki.js backend and the OS webview. The backend runs QuickJS with no JIT, and a tight numeric loop over 96 megabytes of pixels per photo is exactly where a JIT matters. So all the math runs in the page, in Web Workers, and the backend only writes the output file.

One gotcha: Chromium, which WebView2 uses, blocks workers loaded from file URLs, so the worker gets built from function source as a Blob URL. The output goes back to the backend in 3-megabyte base64 chunks because the bridge is text.

The latest change spreads alignment and merging over a pool of helper workers. Four real 24-megapixel JPEGs went from 31.5 seconds to 14.1 seconds on a 24-core machine, and the output matches the single-threaded version pixel for pixel. That shipped in version 0.4.0, along with EXIF in PNG output.

For shipping it, GitHub Actions builds every platform from a tag, plus Linux AppImages. Every release file has a GitHub build attestation, and the checksum file and AppImages are GPG-signed. You can verify the attestation and signatures using specific commands found on the blog post.

What's left? I have only run it on Linux. The macOS, Windows, and Linux ARM64 builds come out of CI and nobody has launched them yet. If you try one, open an issue on the project page and tell me what broke.

The open item that matters most is a real tripod burst on the A6400 in RAW, six to eight frames. That would replace the simulated RAW numbers with real ones.
