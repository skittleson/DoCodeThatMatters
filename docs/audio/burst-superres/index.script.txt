I built photo super-resolution without AI. I wrote the first draft of this post, and while AI helped me edit and rewrite it, I made the final pass.

To give you a quick summary: out of eight thousand, eight hundred and nine Sony photos in my Immich library, exactly one burst was steady enough to merge cleanly. That single number changed what the app had to be. Burst Super-Res is free and open source, it runs offline on macOS, Windows, and Linux, and you can find the download link on the blog post.

This post is part of a series on the Sony A6000 and A6400 cameras. If you want to catch up, Part 1 covers modernization, Part 2 covers more hacks, Part 3 covers tethering with a Raspberry Pi, and there is a separate post for research and community resources. This current post is all about Burst Super-Res.

Let's look at what the app does. You drop in a burst of photos of the same still scene. The app aligns them to a fraction of a pixel, merges them, and gives you one image with twice the width and height. So, four photos that are 6,000 by 4,000 pixels become one image that is 12,000 by 8,000 pixels. It reads JPEG, PNG, and Sony ARW raw files, it keeps your EXIF data, and it shows a split slider so you can see what the merge actually did.

There is no AI anywhere in the processing. Every output pixel comes from pixels your camera actually recorded, averaged after alignment. AI upscalers invent detail that looks plausible, but this only uses detail that is really there. That is also why the gain is small. What you get is less noise and cleaner fine edges. You do not get four times the detail, and the app never shows a megapixel number because of that. On simulated bursts with a known answer, the gain over one upscaled photo measured between plus 0.7 and plus 1.3 decibels in Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio. That is real, but modest.

Why did I build it? Part 1 of the Sony series has a super-resolution trick: you shoot four photos, upscale each one by 200 percent, align them, blend two at 50 percent, then blend the two results. On paper, that's 97.2 megapixels. In practice, it looked more like 48.6 megapixels. It worked, but nobody was going to set up a Python environment for it. I wanted a free tool people could just download.

I set a few rules before writing anything. First, no AI in the processing. Every pixel has to come from the photos. Second, measure on real data and publish the numbers, including where a feature hurts. Third, cross-platform without Electron. The app uses a library called tinyjs, which runs the UI in the operating system's webview, so the Linux build is only about 10 megabytes.

I leaned on research and tools, not intuition. Every idea below got measured against a benchmark before it went in. Since a real burst has no "true" high-resolution image to score against, I made one. I took a real 24 megapixel A6000 or A6400 photo as the answer, shifted copies by random sub-pixel amounts, downsampled them two times the way a sensor pixel averages light, added noise, saved as JPEG, then merged them back and scored against the original. The baseline is the same reference photo upscaled two times with Lanczos-3. Same output size, with and without the burst.

The early numbers were quietly wrong. The harness decoded with a library called jpeg-js, which truncates instead of rounding. That shifted every pixel by negative 0.53 levels per round trip and skewed every score. Switching to libjpeg-turbo's djpeg and cjpeg fixed it. Every number in this post comes from that harness, and you can run it yourself if you want to.

The original method is just a mean. The pairwise 50/50 blend from Part 1 works out to a plain average with extra rounding. For four photos, you blend A and B, blend C and D, then blend those two, so every photo ends up at 25 percent. That's a mean, rounded to 8 bits after each stage. For three photos, it's worse, because the weights come out 25/25/50. So the default became a per-pixel mean or median over all aligned photos, with exact weights for any count. The pairwise mode is still in the app so you can compare against the original.

The bigger change was alignment. The Python version matched SIFT features and fit a homography. The new version uses sub-pixel registration instead: an image pyramid, Lucas-Kanade on tiles, then an affine fit through the tile shifts. On simulated bursts, it lands within 0.03 pixels, including bursts rotated 0.5 degrees.

I tried true shift-and-add, which places every input pixel at its exact sub-pixel spot on the 2x grid. On synthetic test patterns with heavy aliasing, it beat the mean by 1.0 to 1.7 decibels. On real photos, it tied the mean, and on a detailed landscape crop, it was 0.2 to 0.5 decibels worse. So I dropped it.

You need a tripod, don't forget it. The early version only had simulated bursts to show, so I went looking for real ones in my library. I pulled metadata for every Sony photo: 1,880 from the A6400 and 6,929 from the A6000. Shots taken seconds apart with identical settings gave 732 burst candidates, and I ran the app's own alignment over 348 of them. 302 were handheld, more than 10 pixels off. Exactly one was steady: a 13-shot A6000 canyon burst taken in December 2023 at 20 millimeters, f/11, ISO 100. That's the photo at the top of this post. Even that one had three frames that didn't line up well enough for the early version.

There is an image here showing one photo upscaled two times next to five real photos merged. It shows a 200 percent crop, where the left side is the single upscaled photo and the right is the merged result. The merged version has less noise and fewer JPEG block edges.

That's 302 of 348 bursts shot handheld, from someone who wrote a guide on this. A tool that only works on a tripod is a tool for almost nobody.

So, I borrowed from tools that already solved this. I looked at DeepSkyStacker, which scores every frame and stacks only the best ones. The early version warned about bad frames and made you untick them by hand. I looked at Lightroom, which uses specific keys to toggle before/after views. Photographers already know those keys. I looked at Topaz Gigapixel, which compares multiple results with one shared navigator. I looked at Sony's own pixel-shift workflow, which tells you the shooting rules and the wait time up front. And I looked at a Google paper on Handheld Multi-Frame Super-Resolution from 2019. That paper treats hand tremor as the source of useful sub-pixel shifts, as long as each region gets aligned on its own.

That last one changed the plan. The new version added local alignment. When the global fit is worse than 0.1 pixels, a grid of 64 pixel cells gets measured and corrected on top of it. On a real handheld pair where the camera moved 52 pixels, and the near cactus and far hills shifted by different amounts, the error went from 4.36 pixels—which looks like a doubled cactus—to 0.06 pixels. All eight canyon frames came in at 0.22 pixels or better.

There is an image showing the same handheld pair merged with one shift and rotation on the left, and with local alignment on the right. The left side is 4.36 pixels off, while the right side is only 0.06 pixels off.

This version also picked up the Lightroom keys, added badges for good, soft, or too far off photos, automatic unticking of bad frames, and masking of 64 pixel areas where something moved, like water or leaves, so they don't ghost. When a burst really is too far gone, the app says so instead of pretending.

I then went through the research on alignment and stacking, and measured the cheap ideas before adopting any of them. For a four-frame simulated burst, a single photo upscaled two times scores about 33.5 decibels on an A6000 landscape. A mean merge gets you to 34.58. Adding a gentle deblur gets you to 35.54. A sigma-clipped mean is about the same as the plain mean. Deblurring one photo alone actually makes it worse, dropping to 32.69.

Deblurring only helps after the merge. That matches the two-step approach in a 2004 paper by Farsiu et al., which says stacking lowers the noise enough for deconvolution to work. On a single photo, it made things worse. Sigma clipping, which is an astro stacking staple, didn't help because the per-area masking already handles moving things.

That turned into three features in version 0.3. First, sharpening after the merge. It uses a gentle Richardson-Lucy deconvolution. "Auto" picks the amount by leaving one photo out of the merge and choosing whatever predicts that photo best. It's off by default for camera JPEGs because they are already sharpened in camera, and auto measured up to 0.9 decibels worse on those. Second, brightness matching. Exposure flicker gets measured per photo and color evened out. With plus or minus 5 percent simulated flicker, it recovered the true gains to within 0.001. Third, RAW support for Sony ARW files. The point is that a camera JPEG has already thrown away the fine aliasing detail super-resolution needs. So with six or more ARW files, every output pixel gets built straight from the raw sensor samples of all the photos, with no demosaicing in between. The ARW decoder is written from scratch in JavaScript and matches LibRaw on all 24.2 million samples of a real A6000 file. In simulations, RAW scored 0.9 to 2.5 decibels better than merging the same burst as JPEGs.

I have exactly one real ARW to test with and no real RAW burst. So every RAW quality number above is from simulated bursts.

Deep-learning burst super-resolution tops the benchmarks, but it invents detail and needs a big model or a GPU, which breaks the first rule. If you want to go deeper on the classical side, there are resources like the IPOL re-implementation of Google's method and a paper called CANDID that covers optical-flow alignment for scenes with a lot of depth.

Where does the pixel work run? Tinyjs apps have two halves: a backend and the OS webview. The backend runs QuickJS with no JIT, and a tight numeric loop over 96 megabytes of pixels per photo is exactly where a JIT matters. So all the math runs in the page, in Web Workers, and the backend only writes the output file. One gotcha: Chromium blocks workers loaded from file URLs, so the worker gets built from function source as a Blob URL. The output goes back to the backend in 3 megabyte base64 chunks because the bridge is text. The latest change spreads alignment and merging over a pool of helper workers. Four real 24 megapixel JPEGs went from 31.5 seconds to 14.1 seconds on a 24-core machine, and the output matches the single-threaded version pixel for pixel. That shipped in version 0.4.0, along with EXIF in PNG output.

GitHub Actions builds every platform from a tag, plus Linux AppImages. Every release file has a GitHub build attestation, and the checksum file and AppImages are GPG-signed. You can verify them using command line tools if you want to be extra sure.

What's left? I have only run it on Linux. The macOS, Windows, and Linux ARM64 builds come out of CI, but nobody has launched them yet. If you try one, open an issue and tell me what broke. The open item that matters most is a real tripod burst on the A6400 in RAW, six to eight frames. That would replace the simulated RAW numbers with real ones.
